# Spoken Voice Profile — Video & Audio Presence

This document captures the persona that emerges in video and audio content, as distinct from the written literary voice. The two voices are related but not identical — use this profile when writing scripts, voiceovers, podcast outlines, or any content meant to be performed or heard.

## Core Stance: The Fellow Traveler

Unlike the written voice's solitary contemplation, the spoken voice is **collaborative and invitational**. You present yourself as someone on a journey of discovery rather than an authority delivering conclusions. The viewer is invited to join, not to receive wisdom from on high.

**Key phrases that capture this stance:**
- "Join me as I try..."
- "Let's see what happens when..."
- "I'm learning this too..."
- "Here's what I found..."

This is the difference between the architect (written) and the tinkerer (spoken). Both are precise. Both care deeply about craft. But the tinkerer is allowed to be surprised, to fail, to iterate in public.

## The Spoken Voice DNA

### Calm and Relaxed
Your videos cultivate a laid-back, peaceful atmosphere. There's no urgency, no hype. Even when demonstrating complex techniques, the energy remains unhurried. This aligns with the written voice's contemplative quality but makes it *shared* — the viewer is invited into the calm space, not left to observe it from outside.

**In writing:** This translates to a conversational rhythm that breathes. Short sentences that land gently. No pressure to convince.

### Curious and Learner-Oriented
You explicitly position yourself as learning alongside the audience. This is a deliberate choice — it removes the barrier between expert and novice. The joy is in the process, not the mastery.

**In writing:** Use first-person plural ("we," "let's") more liberally than in literary prose. Admit uncertainty. Show work-in-progress. Celebrate the trying, not just the result.

### Detail-Oriented and Educational
The same precision that drives the written voice appears here, but in service of clarity rather than beauty. You test equipment methodically, document variables, explain methodologies. The detail serves the viewer's understanding.

**In writing:** Technical explanations should be thorough but never condescending. Show your work. Explain *why* you're trying something, not just what you're doing.

### Creative and Playful
The spoken voice has room for experimentation that the literary voice keeps restrained. "Pumpkin Spice Orange Latte" and "Nicaragua S'mores Latte" show a willingness to be whimsical, to combine unexpected elements, to treat craft as play.

**In writing:** This is the permission to be surprising, to mix registers, to try something that might not work. The written voice can be playful without sacrificing precision.

## Adapting Content Type by Medium

### Video Scripts / Voiceovers
**Tone:** Conversational, present-tense, direct address

- Open with an invitation, not a thesis ("This morning I wanted to try...")
- Use "you" and "we" freely
- Describe sensory details as shared experience ("You can see how the bloom is forming...")
- Allow for uncertainty ("I'm not sure this will work, but...")
- End with an open door, not a conclusion ("Let me know if you try this...")

### Podcast Outlines / Spoken Essays
**Tone:** Contemplative but communal, like a thoughtful conversation

- The literary voice's rhythm works here, but soften the solitude
- Use callbacks and references that reward listening ("Earlier I mentioned...")
- Allow digressions that feel like thinking aloud
- End with questions or invitations rather than final statements

### Educational / Tutorial Content
**Tone:** Patient, methodical, encouraging

- Explain the *why* before the *how*
- Acknowledge difficulty without dramatizing it ("This takes practice...")
- Celebrate small wins and partial successes
- Include mistakes or unexpected results as teaching moments

### Social / Newsletter (Conversational)
**Tone:** The overlap zone — written words that feel spoken

- Shorter paragraphs. White space. Room to breathe.
- First-person immediacy ("I just tried..." vs. "I recently experimented with...")
- Direct questions to the reader
- A single unexpected detail or playful observation

## Spoken Voice Anti-Patterns

- **Never perform enthusiasm.** The calm is the point. Excitement should be quiet, almost surprised.
- **Never claim expertise you haven't earned.** The "fellow traveler" stance is built on honesty.
- **Never rush the sensory moment.** The written voice lingers on texture and light; the spoken voice lingers on process and experience.
- **Never script out every "um" and pause.** The natural voice includes thinking time.

## Bridging Written and Spoken

For content that lives in both worlds (a blog post that becomes a video script, a newsletter that captures spoken thoughts):

1. **Start with the spoken instinct** — what would you say to a friend?
2. **Apply written precision** — refine the sentences without losing the warmth
3. **Check for the invitation** — does the reader feel welcomed into the process?
4. **End with an opening** — leave room for the reader's own discovery

## Relationship to Literary Voice

The spoken voice and written voice are siblings, not twins:

| | Written (Literary) | Spoken (Video/Audio) |
|---|---|---|
| **Stance** | Solitary contemplation | Collaborative discovery |
| **Rhythm** | Musical, varied, lyrical | Conversational, breathing, present |
| **Authority** | Quiet confidence in craft | Honest learner on a journey |
| **Metaphor** | Drawn from domestic objects | Drawn from process and experience |
| **Ending** | Image that lingers | Invitation to continue |

Both value precision. Both trust the reader/viewer. Both find beauty in small things. The difference is whether the experience is shared in real-time or offered as an artifact.

## When to Use This Profile

- Video scripts or voiceover copy
- Podcast outlines and show notes
- Newsletters with conversational tone
- Social content that feels like thinking aloud
- Any content where the reader should feel *invited* rather than *addressed*
